# School geography

How Minneapolis Public Schools divides the city among its buildings, which schools draw from everywhere, and where the empty seats are. This is the lay of the land behind the [Buildings](facilities.html) page and the school-closure debate. Boundaries are the state's official files for each school year; seats and students are the district's 2024-25 building table. Numbers marked *estimate* are our own arithmetic, not district figures. For orientation, the maps show the Mississippi River, the larger lakes, and I-35W, I-94 and I-394 [15][16].

## The short version

- **Every address has an assigned school at each level.** The city is split into 27 elementary attendance areas. These group into 7 middle school areas and 7 high school areas, so each home points to one elementary, one middle and one high school, a "pathway" [2][5].
- **Not every school has an area.** Nine elementary and K-8 schools, Franklin middle school and FAIR high school are citywide magnets that fill by lottery from anywhere in the city. Andersen is both a magnet and a community school. A few specialty and alternative sites also have no area [5][7].
- **The area is what decides busing.** Elementary students get a bus if they live at least half a mile from their area school, middle school students at least a mile. High school students get a Metro Transit pass. A student who picks a community school outside their own area gets no district busing; magnet students are bused from anywhere in the city [6][7].
- **The current map dates from 2021.** The board's 2020 redesign moved magnets toward the center of the city, ended most K-8 schools, and lined up high school areas with middle school areas. It took effect in 2021-22 [9][10].
- **The empty seats are concentrated in North Minneapolis.** Buildings inside the North and Camden high school areas are about half full; those inside the Washburn area are 79% full [1][2].
- **An empty building does not mean few children live nearby.** About 4 in 10 Minneapolis students who attend public school go to a charter or another district (see [Enrollment](enrollment.html)), and about 5,300 MPS students, roughly 1 in 5, attend a citywide magnet building rather than an area school [1]. A natural follow-up is how many children living in each area attend its school; district or MDE enrollment counts by student home address would show this.

## How school assignment works

- **Community schools** serve an attendance area. Students living in the area enroll there and get district transportation if they live outside the walk zone [6].
- **Magnet schools** teach a theme (arts, STEM or STEAM, Montessori, global studies, or Spanish dual language) and serve "students who choose a particular area of focus" rather than a neighborhood. Seats are filled by lottery, and elementary, K-8 and middle school magnet students get free district busing from anywhere in the city [7]. The district's walk-zone page lists Bethune, Ella Baker, Emerson, Green Central, Hall, Las Estrellas, Marcy, Seward and Sullivan (elementary and K-8), Franklin (middle), and Anishinabe (a citywide program inside the Sullivan building) as citywide, and Andersen as a "citywide magnet & community school" [5].
- **Specialty and alternative sites** such as River Bend, Harrison and Longfellow serve students placed there for particular needs and have no area.
- **Choosing a school outside your area** is allowed when there is space, but the family provides transportation [6][8]. The formal rules are in board Policy 5262 and Regulation 5262A [13].
- **Walk zones** are half a mile for grades Pre-K to 5 and one mile for grades 6-8, shorter in some places where streets are hazardous. High school students who qualify get a Go-To transit pass instead of a yellow bus [6].

## The map

{{chart:elementary}}

- **The least full areas are on the north side.** Hmong International (34% full), Cityview (36%), Nellie Stone Johnson (40%) and Jenny Lind (48%) are the four emptiest community elementary schools in the city [1].
- **The fullest are scattered.** Loring (91%), Pratt (89%), Kenwood (89%), Whittier (88%) and Northrop (87%) are the only community elementary schools at 85% or more [1].
- **Areas differ a lot in size.** Hmong International's area is 0.6 square miles and Pillsbury's is 6.3, partly because some areas include parks, industrial land and downtown [2].
- **Where two buildings split the grades,** as at Lake Harriet, Lake Nokomis, Hale and Field, and Hiawatha and Howe, the area is shaded by the two buildings together. Lake Harriet is 54% full this way: the lower campus is 65% and the upper 44% [1].
- **Year caveat.** Boundaries are for 2025-26 and enrollment is October 1, 2024. The district has proposed changes to many of these boundaries as part of school transformation; none take effect for 2027-28 after the board's October 13, 2026 vote [8][12].

{{chart:middle_high}}

- **Middle and high school areas line up one to one:** Anwatin feeds North, Olson feeds Camden, Northeast feeds Edison, Andersen feeds South, Sanford feeds Roosevelt, Anthony feeds Southwest and Justice Page feeds Washburn [2].
- **North High and Anwatin Middle are both about 40% full.** Washburn High (86%) and Justice Page Middle (81%) are the fullest in their levels [1].

## Where the seats are

{{chart:by_high}}

- **North and Camden have the most empty seats.** The buildings inside the North area have about 6,650 seats for 3,360 students, and the North area also holds four of the citywide magnets (Bethune, Emerson, Hall and Franklin). Camden's seven buildings have about 5,190 seats for 2,640 students [1][2].
- **Together those two areas hold about 5,850 of the district's roughly 16,000 empty seats**, a bit over a third, in about a quarter of the city's area (estimate, from the table above).
- **This counts buildings where they sit, not where students live.** Some students living in the North and Camden areas attend magnets, charters or schools elsewhere, and some cross in the other direction; counts by student home address would show how many. Until those are in hand, these numbers show where the space is, not where the children are.

## How the map changed in 2021

{{chart:before_after}}

- **The 2020 redesign (the Comprehensive District Design)** was approved 6-3 in May 2020 and took effect in 2021-22 [9]. It redrew community school boundaries, converted several south-side magnets to community schools, moved magnets toward the center of the city, and largely replaced K-8 schools with K-5 and 6-8 schools [9][11].
- **About 29% of the city's land was assigned to a different high school** (16.4 of 57.5 square miles), and about 36% to a different elementary school (estimate, our overlay of the state files [2][4]). These are shares of land, not of students.
- **The biggest high school moves:** the west-central part of the old Southwest area, around today's Kenwood elementary area, moved to North (4.2 square miles), and two pieces of the old South area went to Edison (2.5) and Roosevelt (2.3). Henry (now Camden) and North also traded about a square mile each [2][4].
- **Elementary areas went from 22 to 27** in the state files, and every middle school area now sits inside a single high school area. In 2020-21 several elementary areas fed a mix of middle schools [4].
- **It moved programs rather than closing buildings.** The redesign was expected to save about $7 million a year in transportation; no building savings were projected [10]. Enrollment fell 7.6% in its first year (see [Enrollment](enrollment.html)).

## Open questions to check

- **Where students live.** The number of school-age children, and of MPS students, living in each attendance area, and how many of them attend their area school, a magnet, a charter or another district. This would help show whether a half-empty building has few children nearby or few who choose it; enrollment counts by home address, alongside census counts of school-age children by area, would answer it.
- **Boundary history in detail.** The state files go back to 2010-11 and can show each year's changes; this page compares only 2020-21 with 2025-26.
