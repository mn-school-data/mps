# How the district's money is organized

A guide for readers who know business accounting. Figures are for FY2025 (July 2024 to June 2025), the latest audited year, from the district's annual comprehensive financial report (ACFR) [1].

## The short version

- **There is no single P&L.** A school district keeps its books as a set of separate *funds*, each a self-balancing set of accounts for money that is legally tied to a purpose. Bond money can only build buildings, the debt levy can only pay debt, and meal money can only feed students. Think of a holding company whose subsidiaries cannot freely move cash between each other [1].
- **The General Fund is the operating business.** It holds about three quarters of all spending and nearly all of the classroom, special education, transportation and central office costs. It is where deficits, cuts and the reserve policy play out, which is why most of this site focuses on it [1].
- **"Fund balance" is not equity.** It is closer to *working capital that the law lets you spend*. Long-term debts such as pensions and bonds sit outside the funds and appear only in a second set of statements [1].
- **Revenue is mostly set by formula and by levy, not by sales.** The state pays aid per pupil, and local property taxes are capped by state law and by voter-approved referendums [1][3].

## The funds

| Fund | What it is for | FY2025 revenue | FY2025 spending | Fund balance, June 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| General Fund | Day-to-day operations: teaching, special education, transportation, administration, building upkeep | $658.5M | $705.3M | $111.6M |
| Building Construction (capital projects) | Major construction and renovation, paid for mostly with bond proceeds | $9.6M | $92.2M | $219.2M |
| Debt Service | Collects the debt levy and pays bond principal and interest | $113.0M | $104.7M | $56.9M |
| Food Service | School meals, funded by federal and state meal reimbursements and meal charges | combined $57.6M | $27.7M | $1.9M |
| Community Service | Community education, early childhood, adult programs, after-school care | (with Food Service) | $37.4M | $9.8M |

Source: ACFR FY2025, statement of revenues, expenditures and changes in fund balances of governmental funds, and statement of net position [1].

Things to know about the table:

- **Building Construction spending is mostly financed, not earned.** Its revenue is only interest income. In FY2025 it borrowed $86.6M (bonds plus premium) and spent $92.2M on projects, so its large balance is mostly unspent bond money [1].
- **The General Fund subsidizes meals.** Food Service lost nearly $6M in FY2025, and the General Fund transferred $6.2M to cover it [1].
- **Two more funds sit outside the table.** An *internal service fund* runs the district's self-insurance for health, dental, workers' compensation and property and liability risks. It charges the other funds premiums, spent $88.9M in FY2025 and had $8.5M of net position. A *fiduciary fund*, an irrevocable trust for retiree health benefits (OPEB), held $30.0M [1].
- Small statutory accounts such as the MERF pension bond account are folded into these funds in the ACFR [1].

## Two sets of books

The ACFR presents the same district twice [1]:

- **Fund statements** (modified accrual). Each fund reports what it spent and received in the year, with revenue counted only when it is collected within 60 days of year-end. Buying a building counts as spending in full the year it happens. Borrowing shows up as an "other financing source", much like revenue. This view answers *can the district pay its bills this year?* and is the basis for budgets and for state compliance.
- **Government-wide statements** (full accrual). This is the view closest to a corporate balance sheet and income statement: capital assets are depreciated, and long-term debt, pension and retiree health liabilities are recorded. At June 30, 2025 the district had about $923M of capital assets, $1,491M of long-term liabilities (of which $490M is its share of the state teacher and public employee pension plans) and a **total net position of −$216.5M**. The unrestricted part was −$559.3M [1].

The negative net position is common for school districts and does not mean insolvency in the corporate sense. Most of it reflects pension obligations that accounting rules allocate to employers but that are paid over decades through state-set contribution rates [1]. It is still a real long-term cost.

## Fund balance, the closest thing to equity

General Fund balance is split into categories that say how freely it can be spent [1]:

| Category | Meaning | General Fund, June 2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Nonspendable | Not cash, e.g. prepaid items and inventory | $6.1M |
| Restricted | Tied to a purpose by state law or a grantor, e.g. operating capital, literacy aid | $31.9M |
| Committed | Set aside by formal board action | $0 |
| Assigned | Earmarked by management, e.g. next year's planned use | $19.2M |
| Unassigned | Truly free reserve | $54.5M |
| **Total** | | **$111.6M** |

The board's policy targets an unassigned balance of at least 8% of spending, and the district was below it at June 2025 [1]. State law adds a floor: if the General Fund deficit, measured under the state's accounting rules, passes 2.5% of operating spending, the district goes into *statutory operating debt* and must follow a state-approved plan to get out [4].

## Where the revenue comes from

In the General Fund for FY2025 [1]:

- **State aid: $445.1M (68%).** Mostly *general education aid*, a per-pupil formula ($7,281 per pupil in FY2025) with extra weights for poverty, English learners and other factors, plus special education aid [1][3].
- **Property taxes: $146.7M (22%).** These include voter-approved operating referendums and levies the state formula authorizes. A levy certified in December pays for the *next* fiscal year, so the balance sheet carries $253.8M of "property taxes levied for subsequent year" as a deferred inflow rather than revenue [1][3].
- **Federal: $45.5M (7%).** Title I, special education (IDEA) and, through FY2024, the pandemic relief (ESSER) money whose end explains much of the recent drop [1].
- **Other local revenue: $21.3M (3%).** Fees, grants, interest and similar items [1].

There is little a district can do to raise revenue on its own beyond enrollment and asking voters for a referendum, which is why enrollment is central to this analysis.

## UFARS: the state's chart of accounts

UFARS (Uniform Financial Accounting and Reporting Standards) is the chart of accounts and set of rules that every Minnesota school district must use, under Minn. Stat. 123B.77 [2][5]. Every transaction carries a code with these parts: fund, organization (usually the school or department), program (such as regular instruction or special education), finance (the funding source or grant), course, and object (what was bought) or source (where the revenue came from) [5].

Districts report their UFARS data to the Minnesota Department of Education (MDE), which uses it for aid calculations, the statutory operating debt test and its public district finance reports [5]. The ACFR includes a table reconciling the audited figures to what was reported in UFARS. For FY2025 the General Fund matched within about $2M on revenue and $1M on spending [1].

The FY2025 audit found that the district had not followed UFARS rules in some instances (finding 2025-004) and that its budget reporting did not follow UFARS or GAAP (2025-003). The district accepted both findings and has a corrective plan due November 2026 [1].

## The district's accounting system

The general ledger, purchasing and accounts payable run on **SAP**. The FY2025 audit's top finding (2025-001, a material weakness) was about SAP user rights: some employees could create a vendor, enter an invoice and cut a check, and some could post journal entries without review [1]. The ACFR's software subscription note also lists SAP Analytics Cloud from July 2024, and other systems such as Tyler Technologies for student transportation [1]. The public documents do not say when SAP was adopted or how payroll and HR are run.
