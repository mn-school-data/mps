# Meal funding

Several funding streams depend on how many students qualify for free or reduced-price school meals, so the meal count affects revenue streams beyond food service. The largest is Minnesota's compensatory education revenue, which the state pays to each school based on its count of low-income students. Federal Title I money is also aimed at low-income students, and the federal and state meal programs pay for school food itself. This page follows these streams from FY2013 through the latest estimates and asks how much they have to do with the district's recent deficits. Figures marked *estimate* are our own arithmetic, not district or state figures.

## The short version

- **Compensatory revenue is the big one.** It was $51.3M in FY2025, about 8% of general fund revenue [1][5]. It peaked at $59.2M in FY2016, fell every year to a low of $35.2M in FY2023, and then recovered to $55.7M in FY2026 (preliminary) [1].
- **The count of students drives it.** The share of MPS students the state counted as low-income (free plus half of reduced-price, as a share of enrollment) went from 63% for FY2013 to 57% for FY2019, 46% for FY2023, and back to 54% for FY2024 [1]. The FY2023 low reflects fall 2021, when meals were free for everyone and few families returned meal forms. The recovery followed Minnesota adding Medical Assistance (Medicaid) to its automatic matching in fall 2022 [3].
- **Federal money has been steadier.** Title I spending ran $21M to $28M a year, and federal child nutrition reimbursements $14M to $19M [6][7].
- **The meal fund now leans on the general fund.** Food service paid for itself through FY2021. Since FY2022 it has spent $3.5M to $6M a year more than it took in, and the general fund transferred $16.0M to cover it over FY2023 to FY2025 [5].
- **So how much of the deficit is meal-linked?** Some, but not most. In FY2025, when the general fund balance fell $40.6M, the $6.2M transfer to food service was about 15% of the drop, and compensatory revenue actually rose $3.3M that year [1][5]. Compensatory revenue does sit lower than it would if the counted share had stayed at its FY2016 level, by roughly $8M a year in FY2025 and FY2026 (*estimate*, method in the table below). For FY2027, the state's estimate is $2.2M below FY2026, small next to a budget gap the district put at $37M to $50M [1][8].

## How students get counted

A student counts if the family qualifies for free meals (income at or below 130% of the federal poverty guideline) or reduced-price meals (130% to 185%) [3][4]. There are two ways in:

- **Direct certification.** The state matches enrollment records against families already enrolled in SNAP, MFIP (Minnesota's cash assistance program) and, since fall 2022, Medical Assistance, and certifies those children automatically. Children who are homeless, in foster care or migrant also qualify automatically [3].
- **The application.** A family fills out a form listing household size and income. No documents are attached, but each fall districts must check a small sample of approved forms, about 3%, chosen from those near the income cutoff. Families asked for proof who do not respond lose the benefit [4].

Since 2023-24, Minnesota schools serve free breakfast and lunch to every student, whatever the family income [2]. That removed the main reason families had to fill out the form. MPS still asks for it (as the "Educational Benefits" form), because the counts drive other money.

## Compensatory revenue over time

Compensatory revenue is calculated school by school. A school's count is its free-eligible students plus half its reduced-price students on October 1 of the prior year, and the money per counted student rises with the school's concentration of low-income students [3]. The state's rules say how the money can be used, broadly on extra help for students who are behind, and most of it has to stay in the school that generated it [3]. MPS says it now puts all of its compensatory revenue into school allocations, and that it has used a significant amount of it to keep class sizes down [8].

Two things moved the line: the number of students counted, and the dollars per counted student, which follow the state's basic formula. From FY2016 to FY2023 the count fell from 22,707 to 13,776 while the dollars per counted student stayed near $2,600, so revenue fell [1]. In FY2024 both rose: the count recovered to 15,885 and dollars per counted student jumped to about $3,000 [1].

{{chart:share}}

The share counted was already drifting down before the pandemic, from 62% to 57% between the FY2016 and FY2019 counts [1]. Whether that reflects fewer low-income families enrolling in MPS, fewer forms coming back, or both, is an open question.

## What changes in FY2027

Under current law, from FY2027 only students found through direct certification count. Paper applications no longer do [3][9]. The state also guarantees a statewide total of at least $857.2M, scaling every school up if the formula comes in short [9]. For FY2025 and FY2026 the legislature held schools harmless at their FY2024 counts [9].

For MPS most of the count already came from direct certification. In fall 2024, 13,661 students were counted directly and 2,491 through paper forms (15%) [10]. MDE's current estimate puts FY2027 at $53.5M, about $2.2M below FY2026, before a separate FY2027 hold-harmless amount that is not included here [1]. MDE projects $48.1M for FY2028 [1]. In February 2026 the district said the state's estimate came in $3.1M below its initial FY2027 projection, and in July 2026 it added $0.8M of additional compensatory revenue to the budget [8][11].

## Federal money

{{chart:federal}}

Title I is federal money for schools with many low-income students. MPS sends it to schools where at least 35% of students qualify for Educational Benefits, a threshold lowered from 45% for 2025-26 [12]. The district's federal allocation is driven mainly by Census poverty estimates rather than meal forms. The child nutrition line is the USDA's reimbursement for meals served, plus commodity foods and the summer food program [6][7].

## The food service fund

School meals run through their own fund, separate from the general fund. Its revenue is mostly federal meal reimbursement, plus state aid and, before universal free meals, meal sales [5].

{{chart:food}}

Meal sales were about $2M to $2.7M a year before FY2021. They fell under $1M once meals became free for all students, first under federal pandemic waivers and then under the state program [5]. State revenue to the fund rose from under $1M to about $4M in FY2024 with the state's free meals aid [5]. Spending grew from $22.6M in FY2019 to $27.7M in FY2025, and the revenue has not kept up [5].

## How this connects to the deficits

The general fund balance fell $40.6M in FY2025, and the district projected a gap of $37M to $50M for FY2027 while building that budget [5][8]. Most of the FY2025 drop came from the end of federal pandemic relief: federal revenue in the general fund fell from $133M to $45M [5]. Meal-linked money touches the deficits in a few ways:

| Piece | FY2025 | Note |
|---|---|---|
| General fund transfer to food service | $6.2M | Covers the meal fund's shortfall; $16.0M over FY2023 to FY2025 [5] |
| Compensatory revenue, change from prior year | +$3.3M | Rose in FY2025; the big drop was in FY2023 (−$9.3M) [1] |
| Compensatory revenue below the FY2016 counted share | about $8M | *Estimate*: FY2025 enrollment times the FY2016 counted share (61.5%) times FY2025 dollars per counted student, minus actual |
| Compensatory revenue, FY2027 vs FY2026 | −$2.2M | MDE estimate, before the FY2027 hold-harmless [1] |

Compensatory revenue is restricted to eligible uses, so when it falls, the district either trims what it paid for or pays for those staff from general money. The district's use of it for class size means a smaller compensatory check can show up as pressure on the rest of the budget [8].

## Open questions to check

- Why the counted share of MPS students was already falling from FY2016 to FY2019.
- How MPS schools claim federal meal reimbursement (the Community Eligibility Provision or standard counting), and whether that affects revenue.
- What drove food service spending up $5M from FY2019 to FY2025: food, labor, or more meals served.
- How large the FY2027 compensatory hold-harmless is for MPS.
