# Unions and labor contracts

Almost everyone who works for Minneapolis Public Schools is covered by a union contract, and pay and benefits are most of the budget. That makes the contracts, and the unions that negotiate them, central to how the district spends money and runs its schools. For scale: in FY2025 the general fund, which pays for day-to-day operations, spent $705M, of which $545.5M (77%) was salaries and benefits [4]. Since 99% of staff are covered by a contract, roughly three-quarters of operating spending is pay set through collective bargaining. Across all funds, we estimate pay and benefits for union-covered staff at about $600M, about three-quarters of the district's $809M in operating spending and about 63% of its $967M total spending including construction and debt payments (*estimate*) [4]. This page describes who the unions are, how they appear in the budget and in the district's decision-making, and how they connect to state and national union organizations. Figures marked *estimate* are our own arithmetic, not district figures. Figures we found only in press reports, without being able to check the original, are marked *press report*.

## The short version

- **The district bargains with 14 groups.** In FY2025, 5,986 of its 6,036 full-time-equivalent staff (99%) were in a job classification covered by one of them [1][4]. The rest are the school board, the superintendent and about 40 non-represented staff.
- **One union covers most staff.** The Minneapolis Federation of Educators, Local 59, holds three contracts: teachers, education support professionals (ESPs) and adult education teachers. Together they are about 4,850 people and 72% of staff [1][4].
- **Pay and benefits are most of the budget.** Salaries and benefits were $545.5M of $705M in general fund spending in FY2025, or 77% [4]. The district says "80% or more" of its budget goes to wages and benefits [7].
- **Contracts set more than pay.** The teachers' contract sets class-size caps (enforceable from 2026-27), special education caseloads, layoff and placement rules, school-level decision-making teams and several joint labor-management committees [2].
- **The unions are part of larger organizations.** Local 59 belongs to Education Minnesota, the state union, which belongs to both the American Federation of Teachers (AFT) and the National Education Association (NEA). Dues flow up, grants flow back down, and the state union's lobbying shapes the funding and labor laws the district works under [25][26][27].

## How staffing changed by group

{{chart:staff}}

- **Teachers fell 17% from FY2016 to FY2025**, about in line with students counted for state aid (−18%) [4].
- **ESPs rose sharply in FY2023**, while federal pandemic relief was flowing, then fell back in FY2025 [4].
- **The professional and administrator groups grew 38%**, from 494 to 682, and went from 7% to 11% of staff [4]. The district's reports don't say how much of this is new positions and how much is jobs moved between categories. For example, "employment contracts" staff fell from 40 to 9 over the same years. This is one of the open questions below.
- **District bus drivers fell 41%** as the district relied more on contracted buses.

## How contracts show up in the budget

**Raises.** Each round of the teachers' and ESP contracts in recent years:

| Contract | Teachers | ESPs | Cost, as reported |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2021–23, after the March 2022 strike | 2% and 3%, plus a one-time bonus [9] | $2–4 an hour more; the lowest-paid rose to about $35,000 a year [9] | About $53M in 2021-22, paid with $24M of vacancy savings and $31.5M of federal relief, and about $27M more in 2022-23 (*press report*) [10] |
| 2023–25, settled May 2024 | 4% and 5% [11] | 4% and 6%, plus steps [11] | "Within board-approved budget parameters," per the district [11], though district statements differed on whether the deals would widen the budget gap [12]. Reported district analyses put the growth in wages and benefits at about 12% against a 2.5% budget assumption (*press report*) [13] |
| 2025–27, settled November 2025 | 2% and 2%, with a shorter salary schedule [2][6] | Lump sums of $750–$1,750 in 2025-26, then 3% in 2026-27 [3] | About $35M of general fund money over two years, "within our budget assumptions" (*press report*) [15] |

On top of these general raises, teachers move up the schedule for years of service and added credits. In 2025-26 the schedule runs from $54,702 for a new teacher with a bachelor's degree to about $105,000 at the top of the master's-plus-45-credits lane [2].

**Class sizes.** From 2026-27 the teachers' contract requires the district to "provide sufficient resources" to keep classes under set caps. Pre-K through 1st grade is capped at 20 students in schools where 70% or more of students qualify for free or reduced-price meals, and 24 elsewhere. The caps rise to 33 to 36 in middle school and 37 to 38 in high school [2]. During bargaining the district estimated the union's class-size proposal at $20–34M and its own at $14.5M [6]. The FY2027 budget moved $23.6M of compensatory (poverty-based) state aid to "supporting smaller class sizes" [8].

**Benefits and the January 2026 budget update.** Contracts set the district's share of health insurance and a retiree premium reimbursement plan [2]. Pensions are set by state law, not bargaining. When the district's FY2027 gap estimate grew from about $30M to $50.5M in January 2026, "recent contract settlements with bargaining groups" was one of the reasons listed, alongside a higher fringe-benefit rate and extended-time pay [8].

**Union business time.** The teachers' contract gives the union 100 paid days a year for union business, with the union paying for substitutes. Full-time union officers take unpaid leave from the district, and the union reimburses the district for their pay and benefits [2].

## How unions show up in decision-making

**Negotiating and approving.** Under Minnesota's public employee labor law, district staff negotiate, the board is briefed in closed session, union members vote to ratify, and the board approves the contract in public [22][24]. In October 2025, 92% of voting Local 59 members authorized a strike; a tentative agreement was reached before the strike date [6][14]. Teachers can strike only after at least 30 days of mediation and a 10-day notice [23]. The 2025–27 Local 59 contracts were approved 6–0 on December 9, 2025 (*press report*) [16].

**Joint bodies in the teachers' contract** [2]:
- Each school has a *Site Leadership Team*, described as "the policy making body for the school." It includes a budget committee that drafts the school's budget for the team's approval.
- A district-wide *Professional Leadership Team* of 5 teachers (including the union president), 2 ESPs, 5 principals and 2 administrators covers planning time, class size, testing and new initiatives.
- A *Layoff Joint Labor Management Committee*, with equal union and district appointees, reviews staffing data monthly from March to October.

**Layoffs and school changes.** Layoffs follow seniority by license, with an exemption, added in 2022, for teachers from groups underrepresented among a school's licensed staff. Closing or redesigning a school triggers the contract's process for moving teachers to other schools [2]. The US Department of Justice sued over the exemption in December 2025. A federal judge dismissed that case without prejudice in August 2026 because the union was not a party (*press report*) [30]. The clause has not been used, because there have been no teacher layoffs since it was added. When the district presented school closure options in September 2026, the union called them "three different worst-case scenarios" [32]. The board has since ruled out closures for 2027-28 [33].

**School board elections.** Local 59's political fund and the Minneapolis Regional Labor Federation endorse school board candidates, often alongside the DFL party. Examples include Kim Caprini and Josh Pauly in 2018 [19] and Greta Callahan in 2024 [18]. Callahan led the teachers' chapter from 2020 to 2024 and now represents District 6 on the board [21]. For November 2026, a voter-guide site lists Local 59 endorsements of Christin Crabtree (at-large), Abdul Abdi, Shannon Gibney and Becca Dryden [20]. In the state's campaign finance records, the fund's direct contributions to individual board candidates are mostly $600 to $1,000 each. It also supported district levy campaigns, for example $33,500 in 2024, matched by another $33,500 from the union itself [27].

## Where the unions fit: state and national organizations

**What they are legally.** Each union is a tax-exempt labor organization under section 501(c)(5) of the tax code, and under state law it is the certified "exclusive representative" of its bargaining unit [22][25]. Local 59 files an IRS Form 990 each year. Because it represents only public employees, it does not file the federal LM-2 union report [26].

**Money flows both ways.** A full-time member pays local dues plus about $812 a year in 2025-26 for state and national dues and assessments, according to a dues schedule posted by another Education Minnesota local. Education Minnesota passes about $20M a year through to NEA and AFT [26]. Money also comes back down: Education Minnesota gave Local 59 between $0.46M and $0.84M a year from 2016 to 2025, about 11–19% of Local 59's revenue, and NEA and AFT added smaller amounts [25][26]. AFT's largest payments to Local 59 came in 2022, the strike year, and it announced a $100,000 gift to the strike fund [26][31].

{{chart:local59}}

**Sizes.** Local 59 takes in about $3.6–4.2M a year [25]. Education Minnesota takes in about $40M and has about 86,000 members. NEA and AFT together take in more than $600M [25][26]. The other unions are the reverse case. For SEIU Local 284 (7,600 members), Teamsters Local 320 (9,400) and AFSCME Council 5 (27,700), the few hundred MPS staff they represent are a small part of a much larger membership [26].

**Politics and state policy.** Education Minnesota's political committee spent $1–5M a year from 2016 to 2026, mostly through the DFL party and its legislative caucuses [27]. Several 2023 state laws that it supported now show up in MPS's contracts and costs:
- The state's per-student funding formula rose 4% and 2%, then was tied to inflation [28]. MPS's 2023–25 raises came soon after.
- "Student-to-personnel ratios" became a required subject of bargaining for school employees [22]. The 2025–27 contract added enforceable class-size caps. No source we found connects the two directly.
- Teacher pension contributions rose, with partial state aid to offset the cost [29].
- Paid family leave, funded by employers, now adds to the district's benefit rate [8].
- Hourly school workers became eligible for unemployment pay in the summer, paid for by the state [34].

**Shared strategy.** In 2022 the Minneapolis and St. Paul teachers' unions both used a "common good" approach. They bargained over mental health staff, class sizes and recruiting teachers of color as well as pay [9].

## Open questions to check

- What was actual FY2025 payroll by bargaining group? This would replace our estimates and is public data the district could release.
- How much of the growth in the professional and administrator groups since FY2016 is new positions, and how much is reclassification?
- What did each of the last three teacher and ESP settlements cost per year, by the district's own costing?
- How will the 2026-27 class-size caps interact with falling enrollment and any future school consolidation? How often is the contract's space exception used?
- What do the open contracts for clerical, food service, custodial, transportation and administrator staff assume for raises?
- How do board members with close ties to a union approach votes on its contract? The board's conflict-of-interest policy and roll-call records would show this.
- What were Education Minnesota's grants to Local 59 used for? In FY2023 Local 59's net assets fell from $4.4M to $1.4M when a $4.0M receivable from its building corporation came off the books, and the filing does not explain why [25].
