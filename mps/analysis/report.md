# Minneapolis Public Schools: finances, FY2015–FY2027

Compiled 2026-10-15; extended the same day with the audited FY2025 report and the FY2026 budget. Fiscal years end June 30 (FY2024 = the 2023-24 school year). Unless noted, figures are the **general fund**, which is the operating budget (about 70–75% of all governmental spending). Every number traces to a URL in `../sources/*.md`; the cleaned panel is `../data/mps_panel.csv`.

## The short version

1. **Enrollment fell about 20% while spending kept rising.** Students counted for state aid (ADM) dropped from 35,449 (FY2016) to 28,489 (FY2024), then ticked up to 29,109 in FY2025. General fund spending rose from $576M (FY2015) to $700M (FY2024) and $705M (FY2025). Per student that is $16,275 to $24,580 (FY2024), about 14% more after inflation; FY2025 was $24,228, slightly lower because enrollment rose and spending was flat. **Almost all of that real increase came after FY2021.** From FY2015 to FY2021 real spending per student stayed between about $20,400 and $22,400. Then it jumped about 14% in three years (FY2021–2024), while federal relief money was flowing and new labor contracts took effect.
2. **Revenue per student is already high.** In FY2019 MPS took in $20,188 per pupil across all funds against a state figure of $15,701 (32nd highest in Minnesota). Its voter-approved referendum revenue was $1,779 per pupil unit in FY2021, against $768 statewide. By these measures, revenue per student is above the state figure, not below it.
3. **Federal pandemic money filled the gap for a few years.** Federal revenue in the general fund was roughly flat at $38–43M through FY2020, then rose to $59M (FY2021) and $120–133M (FY2022–2024). MPS received about $268M in COVID relief in total. The general fund balance fell to a low of $62.5M in FY2018, then rose to $152M by FY2024. Spending cuts in FY2019 and a bigger property tax take in FY2020 (up $37M) started that recovery, and federal money carried it further. When the relief money ended it fell to **$111.6M at the end of FY2025** (audited), down $40.6M. Federal revenue dropped from $133M to $45M; state revenue rose $45M (special education aid +$30M, English learner aid +$9.4M). The district had budgeted a $92.4M drop; spending came in $30M under budget. The money actually free to spend (assigned plus unassigned) fell from $121M to $74M, slightly below the $79M the June 2025 budget book projected. The press report that FY2025 ended "$28M below expectations" is confirmed: in October 2025 the district's projections still assumed a FY2025 ending balance of $140.3M (preliminary), and the audit came in $28.7M lower at $111.6M.
4. **The operating cushion has often been near or below the board's 8% floor.** The *unassigned* balance is the money actually free to cover shortfalls, and the board's policy floor is 8% of spending. It was 8.6–8.9% in FY2015–2016 and then fell below the floor for three years: 6.8% in FY2017, **5.3% in FY2018** and 6.8% in FY2019. It was 8.3–9.4% in FY2020–2024 ($62M in FY2024) and **fell back below the floor in FY2025**: $54.5M, which the audited report itself puts at 7.5% of FY2026 budgeted spending (it notes a $4.8M prepayment would have kept it above 8%).
5. **The district's own projections show structural deficits of $80–100M a year.** The October 2024 status-quo pro forma had revenue of about $630–638M against spending of $710–724M, putting the general fund at −$14.9M by the end of FY2026 (−2.1%, close to the −2.5% statutory operating debt trigger) and −$447M by FY2030. The district then closed a $75M gap for FY2026, cutting about 90 central-office positions, and voters approved a tech levy worth about $20M. The **FY2026 budget** (adopted June 2025 unchanged from the June 3 proposal) had general fund revenue of $703.9M against spending of $728.4M, with the $24.5M gap covered by one-time money ($11.8M of assigned balance, $5.3M of carryover, $8.4M from the OPEB trust). As **amended on June 9, 2026** it is $719.9M revenue against $729.0M spending and a $11.6M draw on fund balance; the main change is $17.8M more state special education aid than budgeted. The planned $8.4M OPEB trust draw was dropped on the consultant's advice (Sept 2026). No FY2026 year-end figures are public yet: the district says it will report them once the FY2026 audit is far enough along. The **FY2027 budget, adopted June 9, 2026,** is balanced with no use of fund balance: $737.3M revenue, $734.7M spending and $2.6M of transfers out. Of the revenue, $500.8M is state aid (up from $445.1M in FY2025), $173.2M property tax and $53.4M federal. The gap it closed went from $30.3M (Oct 2025 projection) to $50.5M (Jan 2026) to $39.7M (Apr 2026, after the special education recoding). The district's own status-quo projection from October 2025 shows deficits growing from $30M in FY2027 to $141M in FY2031; it started from the preliminary $140.3M FY2025 balance, $28.7M above the audited figure, so an open question is how the later years look when rerun from the audited $111.6M (a later district projection would show this). MPS was not on MDE's statutory operating debt list for FY2023–FY2025.
6. **Staffing grew while enrollment shrank.** Total employees went from 6,486 FTE (FY2015) to 6,644 (FY2024), up 2% while students fell 19%. Teachers fell 5%, from 3,403 to 3,235, so there were 8.8 students per teacher instead of 10.4. Support staff (ESPs) rose 17%, from 1,333 to 1,556. Pupil support spending (transportation and student services) nearly doubled, from $64M to $118M, most of that in FY2022–2024. **FY2025 reversed much of the staffing growth:** 6,036 employees (−608, −9%), 2,952 teachers (−283) and 1,340 ESPs (−216), back to 9.9 students per teacher, and salaries and benefits rose $11M as vacancies were filled.
7. **The FY2025 audit raised findings about financial management.** The FY2025 auditor's opinion is dated March 27, 2026, about three months after the December 31 deadline. The audit reports two material weaknesses (lack of segregation of duties over closing, bank reconciliations and SAP access; vendors paid more than their contracts allowed), a significant deficiency (budgets not coded by UFARS program, which limits how well budget-to-actual reports can be compared), and two compliance findings (not following UFARS; no published notice for the group insurance RFP). Press also reports $5.2M in IRS penalties and years of under-claimed special education aid (about $21.5M recovered over FY2026–2027); those two items have not yet been checked against district documents; board materials or the FY2026 audit would confirm them.

8. **About two-thirds of spending moves with enrollment; one-third does not.** Instruction and instructional support ($475M in FY2025) fell faster than students over FY2016–2025, while administration, transportation and student services, and buildings ($230M) held flat or grew. State aid fell with enrollment until FY2023 and then rose $90M in two years after the 2023 Legislature raised the formulas; per student after inflation it is back to its FY2016 level. The FY2026 budget is about $20M higher, mostly technology-levy supplies. The jumps in pupil support (mostly contracted buses) and special education instruction (mostly a FY2025 recoding out of regular instruction, more than covered by new state aid) are broken down on the [Money in and out](money.html#special-education-and-pupil-support) page.

## Where the money goes (FY2024, $700M)

Regular instruction $291M (42%), special education instruction $118M (17%), pupil support $118M (17%, up from $64M in FY2015, mostly transportation and student services), instructional support $50M, sites and buildings $40M, district support $36M, administration $20M. By object, salaries were $395M and benefits $139M (76% together).

In FY2025 ($705M) special education instruction jumped to $141.6M (from $118.2M) while regular instruction fell to $276.6M (from $290.6M) and pupil support to $108.1M. The shift lines up with the district correcting how special education costs were coded (inferred; the audit does not discuss the cause). Salaries were $401M and benefits $145M.

## Open questions to settle

- **What was the FY2024 year-end balance?** Monthly statements reportedly showed about $90M while the audited total is $152M. The likely explanation is that the $90M is an unassigned or available figure rather than the total, but this needs the source documents.
- **How did FY2026 end?** The amended budget planned an $11.6M draw on fund balance, and the March 2026 statement projected ending the year back above the 8% unassigned policy. Actual year-end figures wait on the FY2026 audit.
- **How much of the FY2021–2024 spending growth is permanent?** That is when federal relief and new labor contracts both took effect. UFARS object/program detail would separate the two.
- **What drove the FY2020 property tax jump** ($114M to $151M)? The likely causes are the 2018 referendum ($490 per pupil) taking effect and levy timing; the levy certifications would confirm it.

## Step 2: comparison district

St. Paul Public Schools is the comparison. It is the only Minnesota district of similar size and urban character. For FY2019, St. Paul's all-fund revenue per pupil was $18,966, against $20,188 for MPS. St. Paul is also running deficits, so the comparison is about how two similar urban districts handled the same decade, not about a "healthy" baseline. A second comparison will be a district of similar size in a far-away, culturally different metro of similar size (for example Salt Lake City, Boise, or one in the Northeast), preferably one with strong test scores.
