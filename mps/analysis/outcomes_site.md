# Tests and college

This page looks at how Minneapolis students do on state tests, how many graduate, and where they go after high school, next to St. Paul, the suburbs and every other district in Minnesota. The college figures follow each high school class into college through the state's longitudinal data system (SLEDS), which also counts students who leave Minnesota, using National Student Clearinghouse data [3]. "First-ring" and "second-ring" suburbs are our own grouping of 16 and 17 metro districts. The districts are listed in the table under the suburb chart. Where several classes are combined, or districts are grouped, the figure is our own graduate-weighted average (*estimate*).

## The short version

- **Test scores have partly recovered since the pandemic.** 35.8% of Minneapolis students tested were proficient in math in 2025 and 37.1% in 2026, against 42.2% in 2019 and 44.5% in 2015. The state is at about 45% [1]. Reading rose from 42% in 2015 to 47% in 2019 and has been at 40% to 42% since. Minneapolis ranks 245th of 325 districts in 2025 math, and St. Paul ranks 302nd.
- **Graduation rose and then dipped.** The four-year rate went from 65% for the class of 2015 to 75% for 2019, and it was 73% in 2025. The state rate is 85% [2].
- **Starting at a four-year college is where Minneapolis stands out.** 45% of the class of 2023 and 44% of the class of 2024 started at a four-year college, a little above the state (43% and 42%) [3]. That is up from 32% for the class of 2020. Over the classes of 2022 to 2024, Minneapolis ranks 107th of 310 districts.
- **Finishing a bachelor's degree lags.** About a third of the classes of 2015 to 2018 have earned one, against about 42% statewide and 45% to 54% in the suburbs [3]. On this measure Minneapolis ranks 203rd of 319.
- **Fewer students start at two-year and technical colleges.** The share fell from about 25% for the classes of 2015 to 2017 to 15% to 16% for 2023 and 2024, now a little below the state [3].
- **Spending and class size don't line up with scores the way one might expect.** Minneapolis spends more per student than almost any other district and has among the fewest students per teacher [5]. Across Minnesota, districts that spend more tend to have *lower* scores, most likely because extra aid follows students with greater needs.

## Test scores

The state tests (MCA) are given every spring: math in grades 3 to 8 and 11, reading in grades 3 to 8 and 10, and science in grades 5 and 8 and once in high school. The figures here are for all grades tested together [1]. From 2019 the state's files also fold in MTAS and the Alternate MCA, which some students with disabilities take instead. They are a small share of students tested (about 2% in Minneapolis and 1.5% statewide in 2019 science, for example), so the 2015-2018 figures, which count the MCA only, are close but not exactly comparable. No tests were given in 2020, and fewer students took them in 2021. Minneapolis tested 13,026 students in math in 2025, against 15,850 in 2019, partly because enrollment fell and partly because more families opted out. The rate counts only students who took the test, so a change in who opts out can move it.

### Math

{{chart:math}}

Math was already drifting down before the pandemic, statewide and in Minneapolis. Minneapolis went from 44.5% proficient in 2015 to 42.2% in 2019, and the state from 60.2% to 55.0% [1]. Scores dropped in 2021 and 2022 and have been rising slowly since in both cities, while the state has been flat. Since 2022, Minneapolis has been about 8 to 10 points above St. Paul.

### Reading

{{chart:reading}}

Reading moved the other way before the pandemic. Minneapolis rose from 41.9% proficient in 2015 to 46.9% in 2019, while the state was close to flat at about 59% to 60% [1]. Since then Minneapolis has been at 40% to 42%. 2026 was the first year of a new reading test (MCA-IV), so part of the last step may reflect the new test rather than a change in students.

### Science

{{chart:science}}

Science has followed the state's downward path, but more gently. Minneapolis went from 36.1% proficient in 2015 to 31.8% in 2024, while the state fell from 53.0% to 39.6% [1]. So the gap to the state narrowed from about 17 points to about 8. A new science test started in 2025, and scores everywhere dropped with it: 22.9% in Minneapolis and 26.2% statewide in 2025, the first year. Comparisons from 2025 on should be made within the new test only. Open question to check: why did the state's science scores fall so much more than Minneapolis's between 2015 and 2024?

### ACT

The ACT is a college admissions test, and SLEDS records whether each graduate took it before graduating and their composite score [4]. Who takes it has changed a lot. For the classes of about 2016 to 2020, the state paid for every junior to take it, and about 80% of graduates statewide did. After the pandemic participation fell, to 34% of the Minneapolis class of 2021.

{{chart:act_took}}

{{chart:act_score}}

Minneapolis graduates who took the ACT averaged about 20 for the classes of 2015 to 2020, against about 21.5 statewide. The jump to 22.5 for the class of 2021 came when only a third of the class took it, so it most likely reflects who took the test, not a change in what students learned. With participation back near 60%, the average has settled at about 21.2, a little above the state (20.6) [4].

## Graduation

{{chart:grad}}

The four-year rate counts students who graduate within four years of starting ninth grade [2]. Both cities dipped in 2023 while the state did not. Open question to check: in Minneapolis's 2023 file, 11.6% of the cohort have an "unknown" status, which is a large share. Was this a reporting change, or did fewer students really finish on time?

## Where graduates go: four-year colleges

{{chart:four}}

SLEDS counts a graduate as enrolled if they start college in the fall after high school, anywhere in the country [3]. The Minneapolis line fell to 32% for the class of 2020, the first pandemic class, and then rose to 45% and 44% for 2023 and 2024. That is about where the first-ring suburbs are (46% to 47%), and above the state. St. Paul has stayed between 33% and 38% throughout.

The University of Minnesota Twin Cities is the most common first college for Minneapolis graduates (260 students from the class of 2024). It is followed by Minneapolis College (156), Augsburg University (63), Normandale Community College (53) and the University of Wisconsin-Madison (46) [4]. About 16% to 17% of Minneapolis graduates go to a four-year college in another state, close to the state rate.

Open question to check: the graduating class also got smaller in these years. Is the higher rate a sign that more students are aiming for four-year colleges, or that a smaller, more college-bound group is graduating? Student-group figures in the SLEDS reports could help separate the two.

## Finishing a degree

{{chart:bach}}

SLEDS counts degrees earned by its latest data year, so older classes have had more time to finish [3]. Compare places within a class year, not across years. The latest class shown is 2018. Minneapolis peaked at 40% for the class of 2012 and has been at 32% to 34% since 2015. The gap to the state, which is about 42%, widened from about 3 points for the class of 2012 to 8 to 10 points.

So more Minneapolis graduates now start at four-year colleges than earn bachelor's degrees a few years later. Open question to check: SLEDS also reports how many starters return for a second year. Comparing those rates with the suburbs would show where the gap opens.

## Two-year and technical colleges

{{chart:two}}

Minneapolis graduates used to start at two-year colleges more often than the state average. That share has fallen faster than the state's, from about 25% to 27% for the classes of 2015 to 2017 to 15% to 16% for 2023 and 2024 [3]. Their most common two-year schools are Minneapolis College (Minneapolis Community and Technical College), Normandale and Saint Paul College [4]. For the class of 2017, 5.4% have earned a certificate and 6.4% an associate degree, against 4.9% and 9.1% statewide [3].

## The suburbs, district by district

{{chart:metro}}

The suburbs are not all alike. Edina, Wayzata, Orono, Minnetonka, Mahtomedi and Eden Prairie send two thirds or more of their graduates to four-year colleges, and about 70% of their 2015-2017 graduates have earned a bachelor's degree. Several inner suburbs to the north and south look like Minneapolis or below it. Columbia Heights, Robbinsdale, Fridley, South St. Paul, Richfield and Brooklyn Center are all between 13% and 35%. So the question of whether families leave Minneapolis for the suburbs depends a lot on which suburb. This page compares each district's own graduates. It does not show how many Minneapolis residents attend suburban districts or charter schools, which the state's open-enrollment files would show.

## Statewide maps

{{chart:maps}}

The highest four-year college rates and math scores are in the west and south metro suburbs, plus a scattering of southern and western rural districts. Rates are lower in many north-central and northeastern districts [1][3].

## Spending, class size and teacher experience

Test scores line up closely with family income, but a district can't change that. So we looked instead at three things a district does decide: how much it spends per student, how many students there are per licensed teacher, and how experienced its educators are [5].

{{chart:choices}}

- **Spending.** Minneapolis spent $22,228 per student in FY2025, 6th highest of the 309 districts with 100 or more students tested. St. Paul spent $21,324 [5]. Across districts, higher spending goes with lower math scores. That is almost certainly because state and federal aid follows students with greater needs (low-income students, English learners, students in special education). So the spending probably follows need, rather than causing low scores.
- **Students per teacher.** Minneapolis has 12.3 students per licensed teacher, among the fewest in the state. St. Paul has 15.0 [5]. Smaller ratios also go with lower scores across districts, for the same likely reason.
- **Experience.** 61.2% of Minneapolis educators have more than three years' experience, among the lowest shares (263rd of 307). St. Paul's share is 65.4% [5]. Across districts, experience lines up with scores only weakly.

Open questions to check: where do Minneapolis's extra dollars and staff go? Special education, English learner services and transportation are likely candidates, and the [Financial structure](structure.html) page breaks down spending by program. Why is the share of experienced educators low, and has it changed since before the pandemic?

## How to read these numbers

- Charts show associations between districts, not causes.
- Pooled figures (classes 2022-2024 for starting college, 2015-2017 for degrees) are our graduate-weighted averages (*estimate*). Pooling keeps small districts from jumping around. Rankings cover school districts only, not charter schools.
- SLEDS out-of-state data starts with the class of 2010, so the four-year and two-year lines start there.
- Spending is the state Report Card's total spending per student (average daily membership served), FY2025. Staffing figures are for 2024-25 [5]. Districts spending over $40,000 per student (a few very small districts) are left out of the spending chart.
