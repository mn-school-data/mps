# Buildings and the school-closure debate

What the district's buildings cost, how full they are, what they cost Minneapolis taxpayers, and what closing some of them could and could not save. Figures are from the district's audited FY2025 financial report and its own school transformation documents unless marked otherwise. Numbers marked *estimate* are our own arithmetic on those figures, not district figures.

## The short version

- **No schools will close for 2027-28.** On October 13, 2026 the board voted not to close or merge schools for that year, as the superintendent had recommended [3]. The underlying reasons (empty seats and aging buildings) have not changed, so the question is likely to return.
- **The schools are about two-thirds full.** In 2024-25, 59 active school buildings had 44,482 seats for 28,319 students, or 64% [1]. The district's own 2024 space study found almost the same, 63% [6].
- **Running buildings is a small share of the operating budget.** The General Fund spent $39.9M on sites and buildings in FY2025, 5.7% of its $705M [1]. That is the only building cost that bears directly on the operating deficit.
- **Most building money comes from separate, restricted pots.** Maintenance, construction and debt are paid mainly from dedicated levies and bonds, which by law cannot be spent on classrooms [1][9]. Shrinking them would lower future property taxes and borrowing, not the operating deficit.
- **Buildings take a bit more than a third of the school property tax.** Of the $280.3M levied for taxes payable in 2026, $102.5M pays building debt and about $5M more pays for maintenance and leases [14]. On a median Minneapolis home that is roughly $420 of about $1,270 a year in school taxes (estimate).
- **Where that tax goes next depends mostly on new borrowing.** Payments on existing debt fall by about half by the early 2030s as old bonds are paid off. At the recent pace of new bonds they would stay near today's level instead (estimate).
- **The district's closure options aimed at goals other than saving money.** They would have saved an estimated $0.2M to $2.4M a year, because the district reinvested staff savings from closed schools in the remaining ones [4][5].

## How full the buildings are

{{chart:utilization}}

- **Empty space by level.** Elementary schools are 68% full, middle and K-8 schools 63%, and high schools 58% [1]. Enrollment fell about 18% from FY2016 to FY2025 (35,449 to 29,109 students counted for state aid), while the number of school buildings stayed about the same [1].
- **Most buildings are well under capacity.** 22 of the 59 buildings are less than 60% full, and only 10 are at 85% or more [1]. Every high school except Washburn is under 62%.
- **This is not new.** The FY2022 report showed 61% (27,115 students in 44,126 seats) [2].
- **How much is "too much" is a judgment call.** If 85% is used as a target, about 11,000 seats, roughly a fifth of school space, are above it (estimate). That is an upper bound, not a plan: seats in one neighborhood or grade level cannot simply serve students in another.
- **Data caveats.** The FY2025 table prints the same square footage for Sullivan as for Waite Park, so the FY2022 figure is used for Sullivan. Several other buildings' square footage differs between the two reports, and 658 online-school students are not in any building [1][2].

## What buildings cost, and who pays

| FY2025, $M | General Fund | Building Construction fund | Debt Service fund | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Running and maintaining buildings | 39.9 | 57.6 | – | 97.6 |
| Capital projects | 7.5 | 29.4 | – | 36.9 |
| Debt principal and interest | 7.5* | 5.2* | 104.7 | 117.4 |
| *Paid from* | state aid and operating levy | bonds and maintenance levy | debt levy | |

Source: FY2025 governmental funds statement [1]. \*Mostly leases and software subscriptions rather than buildings.

{{chart:spending}}

- **General Fund building costs fell after inflation.** They rose 16% from FY2016 to FY2025 while consumer prices rose about 34% [1][8]. The growth in building spending came from maintenance paid out of the Building Construction fund and from debt service.
- **Why total building spending rose while the General Fund's did not.** Building operating spending across all funds is just two pieces: the General Fund and the Building Construction fund. In FY2025 that was $39.9M plus $57.6M, or $97.6M [1]. The Building Construction piece is project work paid from bonds and the maintenance levy and booked as ordinary spending rather than as a new asset, such as repairs and maintenance projects. It moves with the project pipeline rather than day-to-day operations, and it jumped to $57.6M in FY2025 (it was also $57.4M in FY2019) [1].
- **Part of the FY2025 jump looks like accounting rather than more work.** That year the fund's project spending booked as ordinary spending rose $14.3M while its capitalized spending fell about $33M, so the fund's total spending actually fell, from about $106M to $87M (approximate) [1]. A by-project breakdown of this line, which the reports do not include, would show what drove the shift.
- **Per student, the cost rose sharply** because the buildings stayed while students left. Building operating spending across all funds (General Fund plus Building Construction fund) went from about $1,900 to $3,350 per student [1].
- **Debt.** At June 30, 2025 the district owed $638.7M in general obligation bonds and $131.5M in certificates of participation, and it issued another $81.9M of building and maintenance bonds in December 2025 [1].
- **Long-term maintenance plan.** The approved 10-year plan for FY2026-2035 totals $601M, rising from $34M in FY2025 to $60-69M a year [7]. The last completed building condition assessment is from 2017; we have not found a newer one published yet, and it would show the current backlog.

## What closing a building saves

Closing a school affects three kinds of cost, each paid from a different pot:

| Cost | Typical elementary school (about 75,000 sq ft) | Pot | Caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Running the building (custodial, utilities, repairs) | about $360,000 a year (estimate, at $4.80 per sq ft) | General Fund | A building kept vacant still costs $1.22-2.06 per sq ft a year to heat and secure [10] |
| Maintenance and capital avoided | about $550,000 a year (estimate, at the plan average of $7.30 per sq ft) | Maintenance levy and bonds | Only saved if the building is sold or demolished |
| School-level staff needed whatever the school's size (principal, office, social worker, nurse, library) | median about $650,000 a year in the district's own model [5] | General Fund | Only saved if the positions are cut rather than moved |

- **The district's options.** The September 2026 options would have taken 59 schools down to 44 or 45. They counted $3.6-3.7M a year of transportation and utility savings, and reinvested most of it in a minimum staffing model at every remaining school; they assumed no custodial reduction. Net savings were $0.2M to $2.4M a year [4][5]. The district stated that cutting costs was not the goal.
- **A cost-cutting version would save more, but how much is uncertain.** In the district's own staffing model, the 14 to 16 schools in each option carry $9.7-10.9M a year of school-level non-teaching staff [5]. Adding the transportation and utility savings gives roughly **$13-14M a year at most** (estimate). That is an untested upper bound: it leaves out extra staff the receiving schools would need, one-time moving costs, and the cost of holding vacant buildings, and we have not found a costing of this version by the district or others. A school-by-school estimate of receiving-school staffing and transition costs would narrow it.
- **Other districts saved less than projected.** A Pew study of large districts found savings were usually modest and below projections: Milwaukee saved $6.6M a year against $10M projected, Washington DC $17M against $23M [11]. Twelve large cities still held 301 closed buildings in 2012, which keeps carrying costs going [12]. St. Paul's superintendent called its 2021 closure plan "not a major cost saving measure" [13].
- **Selling buildings does not fund classrooms.** Under state law, sale proceeds go mostly to paying down debt, and the district expects sales to take 2-5 years [10].

## What buildings cost Minneapolis taxpayers

### How the school levy is split

| Taxes payable 2026 | Amount | What it pays for | Voter-approved? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Debt service levy | $102.5M | Bonds and lease-purchase certificates for buildings and the maintenance plan, after $11.0M of state maintenance aid | No |
| Maintenance and lease levies in the General Fund | about $5.4M | Health and safety work and building leases | No |
| Technology levy | $36.3M | Technology, not buildings | Yes (2024) |
| Operating referendums | $73.6M | Class sizes and student services | Yes |
| Other operating levies | about $62M | Set by state formula | No |
| **Total** | **$280.3M** | | |

Source: the state's levy certification for taxes payable 2026 [14] and the district's 2027 levy update [16].

- **Most building debt needs no public vote.** A state law specific to Minneapolis lets the board issue building bonds by a two-thirds board vote, up to a yearly cap (5.1% of tax capacity plus $15M) [15]. Maintenance bonds follow the state's maintenance formula [9]. Only the operating referendums and the technology levy went to voters.

{{chart:levy}}

- **The debt levy grew faster than the total.** From taxes payable 2016 to 2026 the debt service levy rose 62% ($63.2M to $102.5M), while the total school levy rose 51% and enrollment fell 18% [1][14]. Building debt per student roughly doubled.
- **The overall tax rate held about steady.** The total levy grew about as fast as taxable market value (+48% from 2016 to 2025), so rising values, not a rising rate, carried most of the increase [1].

### What a typical homeowner pays

This is our estimate, not a district figure. For a median-value $333,000 home [17]:

- **Building debt:** about $420 a year. After the homestead exclusion [18], about $3,164 of tax capacity is taxed at the 2025 debt service rate of 13.35% [1].
- **All school taxes:** about $1,270 a year, including about $520 for the operating referendums, which are taxed on market value. The district says school taxes are about 24% of a typical Minneapolis homeowner's 2026 bill [16].
- **Rule of thumb:** each $10M of debt levy adds about $40 a year on a median home.
- **Caveat:** these use 2025 tax rates on a 2026 home value and ignore credits and refunds, so treat them as approximate.

### Who carries the tax

- **The tax base has shrunk while the levy grew.** Minneapolis's taxable capacity fell from $806.7M (taxes payable 2023) to $762.4M (2025), while the school levy rose 14% [1].
- **Downtown is carrying less.** The ten largest taxpayers, nearly all downtown office towers, made up 2.86% of the tax base in 2025, down from 4.97% in 2016 [1]. When commercial values fall, the same levy shifts toward homes. The district makes the same point: "the less commercial and industrial property, the greater burden on homeowners" [16].
- **Open question:** how far the burden has already shifted toward homes. The homestead share of the tax base by year, from county or state assessment data, would show it.

### Where the debt tax could go

{{chart:debt}}

- **Existing debt runs down quickly.** Scheduled payments on bonds and certificates already issued fall from about $107M in FY2027 to an average of about $55M a year in FY2031-35 [1].
- **New borrowing decides the path.** The district has recently issued about $80M of new bonds a year. If that continues, debt payments stay around $90-118M a year through 2040. If it borrowed only for the maintenance plan (about $38M a year), payments would drift down to about $60-85M. These are our projections, assuming 20-year bonds at 5%, not district figures.
- **Pressures pushing it up.** The maintenance plan ramps to $60-69M a year from FY2028 [7]. A new building for Anishinabe Academy is estimated at $90-105M [19]. The building condition assessment is from 2017, so the size of today's backlog is an open question that the new assessment would answer. The city is also proposing an 11.3% levy increase for 2027, a separate tax on the same bills [20].
- **What could bring it down.** Fewer buildings would shrink the maintenance plan, which drives most of the district's maintenance revenue [9][14]. Slower bond issuance, and sale proceeds, which by law go to debt [10], would also help.
- **Next decision.** The 2027 school levy is set at the Truth-in-Taxation hearing on December 8, 2026; the board set a preliminary maximum on September 8 [16]. The 2026 levy rose 0.42%, and the district says typical increases are about 2-4% [20].

## What this means

- **Buildings did not cause the operating deficit.** General Fund building costs grew less than inflation. The deficit comes mainly from staff costs and revenue (see the [Overview](index.html) and [Money in and out](money.html)). Closing buildings alone cannot close it.
- **The larger building question is long-run capital.** The $601M maintenance plan pays to keep up space that is about two-thirds used. That cost falls on property taxes through levies and bonds, not on the classroom budget. Because the district's maintenance revenue follows its approved plan rather than enrollment, a smaller set of buildings would mainly lower that levy (estimate, based on our reading of the state formula [9]).
- **Open questions to check.** The new building condition assessment, building-by-building running costs, one-time transition costs, and how much custodial and maintenance spending would actually fall with fewer square feet. These would let a reader price closures more precisely.
