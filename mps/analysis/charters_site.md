# Charter schools

Charter schools are public schools run by their own nonprofit boards instead of a school district. They are free, take students from anywhere in the state, and get state aid per student much as a district does. For Minneapolis families they are the largest alternative to MPS. Of roughly 53,000 school-age Minneapolis residents in 2024-25, about 25,500 were not in MPS. By our rough estimate, about 13,800 of them (54%) were in charter schools and other public programs, 6,100 (24%) in other districts through open enrollment, about 5,200 (20%) in private schools, and about 400 (2%) homeschooled [8][12]. This page maps the charter schools in and near Minneapolis, how many students they enroll, what kinds of grades they serve, how many Minneapolis residents go elsewhere, how charter students do on state tests, and how much private money charters raise beyond public aid. It looks at the most recent one or two years, since the aim is to understand today's school-choice landscape. Figures marked *estimate* are our own arithmetic.

## The short version

- **About 26 charter schools have buildings in Minneapolis, and 36 more sit within 3 miles of the city line.** In fall 2025 the in-city buildings held 9,815 students and the nearby ones 15,915 [1][2]. For scale, MPS enrolled 28,814 students in kindergarten through grade 12 that fall [2].
- **About 4 in 10 Minneapolis residents in public school attend somewhere other than MPS.** In 2024-25, of about 47,700 residents in kindergarten through grade 12, roughly 27,800 (58%) were in MPS, 6,100 (13%) open-enrolled in another district, and about 13,800 (29%) were in charter schools and other public options (*estimate*) [8]. That matches the roughly 28% in charters that MDE figures showed for 2022-23 [4]. The share not in MPS is highest in grades 6-8, at 46%.
- **Charters in the city lean toward the middle grades.** Counting students where the building is, charter buildings in Minneapolis enroll about 35 students for every 100 MPS students in grades K-5, 45 in grades 6-8, and 25 in grades 9-12 (*estimate*) [2]. That fits the roughly 11% of each MPS class that leaves between 5th and 6th grade (see [Enrollment](enrollment.html)).
- **Most charters are small.** The median in-city charter enrolled about 320 students across all its buildings. A few are large networks: Hiawatha Academies (1,683), Metro Schools (1,643, about a third online), Twin Cities International (905) and Yinghua Academy (875) [2].
- **Test scores vary widely.** Averaged over 2025 and 2026, students at a few charters scored well above the state average (Yinghua Academy, Nova Classical Academy, Twin Cities German Immersion), while students at most in-city charters scored below MPS's average, which is itself about a third of a standard deviation below the state [7]. These charters serve very different students, so the comparison by student group below is more telling.
- **Private gifts are a small share of charter money, with a few exceptions.** Across the 58 charters with recent tax returns, gifts from private donors added up to about $11.3M, roughly 2% of the $596M the schools took in (*estimate*) [3]. The typical charter raised about $160 per student. A handful raised over $1,500 per student, including KIPP Minnesota, Prodeo Academy and SciTech Academy.
- **Most charter students are in buildings owned by the school's own building company or foundation.** County records show that for about 54% of students at charter buildings inside Minneapolis and 75% just outside it [13]. Twenty-two charters reported building companies with about $427M in liabilities, most of it tax-exempt bonds [3]. That is a long-term commitment to stay in place.

## Where they are

Inside the city, charters cluster in three places: south Minneapolis from Phillips and Powderhorn east to Longfellow, the North Side near Olson Memorial Highway and Broadway, and Northeast. Southwest Minneapolis, west of Lyndale Avenue and south of downtown, has none. Just outside the line there are clusters along University Avenue in St. Paul's Midway, in Columbia Heights, Brooklyn Center and Richfield. Several charters run more than one building, and some have one inside the city and one outside: Prodeo Academy (Columbia Heights and St. Paul), Academias La Paz (El Colegio in Minneapolis, Academia Cesar Chavez in St. Paul) and Universal Academy (Minneapolis and Burnsville).

Two changes since fall 2025 are worth knowing: Agamim Classical Academy closed at the end of June 2026, and KIPP Minnesota became NorthStar Public Schools on July 1, 2026.

## How big they are

{{chart:size}}

Some charters also run online programs, which enroll students from anywhere in the state and are left out of the map and the chart above. They are large: in fall 2025, 17 online programs run by charters based in or near Minneapolis enrolled 8,005 students [1][2]. The biggest is Minnesota Transitions, whose online schools (Minnesota Virtual Academy and Minnesota Connections Academy) enrolled about 5,900 of its 6,425 students.

Enrollment held fairly steady from fall 2024 to fall 2025 at most in-city charters. Apart from Minnesota Transitions (+743, nearly all online), Universal Academy (+159), Metro Schools (+108) and Hiawatha Academies (+97) grew the most; Hennepin Schools (-67), Venture Academy (-57) and Academias La Paz (-58) shrank the most [2].

## Where Minneapolis residents go

{{chart:residents}}

The state counts public school students both where they live (resident district) and where they attend. Comparing the two shows how many Minneapolis residents in public school are not in MPS. Private school counts are by the school's location, not where students live, so the private bar is a rough estimate; the homeschool count is by residence. The state file does not split the remainder by school, so we cannot say how many Minneapolis residents attend each charter; nearly all of the remainder is charter schools, with a small number in programs such as intermediate districts [8].

{{chart:residents_grades}}

The pattern by grade matches the charter seats counted above: the share of residents going elsewhere is highest in the middle grades, lower in elementary school, and in between in high school.

## Who they serve

Most in-city charters serve mainly students of color, and many serve mainly students from low-income families. Of the 26 in-city charters, 18 reported that more than 90% of their students were students of color in fall 2025 [2]. A few are much closer to the city's overall mix, among them Yinghua Academy (66%), New City School (45%) and Cyber Village Academy (40%). Free and reduced-price meal counts are missing for many charters in the state file, because of privacy suppression or because the school serves free meals to everyone and does not collect forms [2].

## Test scores

The Minnesota Comprehensive Assessments (MCA) are given each spring in reading and math in grades 3-8 and once in high school. To compare schools across grades, we placed each score against the statewide average for the same grade, subject and year, measured in statewide standard deviations, and averaged over the 2025 and 2026 tests. A score of 0 is the state average; −1 is a full standard deviation below it, roughly the distance between the 50th and 16th percentile of students statewide [7].

{{chart:tests}}

A few charters near the city, several with language immersion or a classical curriculum, score well above the state. Many of the in-city charters score below MPS's average. They also serve a very different mix of students: most in-city charters enroll mainly students of color and students from low-income families, so a school-wide average mostly reflects who attends. Most charters' scores are also less spread out than the state's (mostly 0.7 to 1.0 state standard deviations, against 1.2 for MPS), which fits schools that serve a narrower range of students. The share of students at each achievement level is in the data file.

### Is it the school, or who enrolls?

A school's average score reflects both what the school does and which students come through its doors. Families who seek out a charter may differ from those who don't, in ways the files cannot see. The selection can run either way, since some families leave a school because their child is struggling there. The cleanest test would compare students who won and lost a charter's admission lottery, which needs student-level records not in the public files. What the public files do offer is year-to-year progress: MDE scores how each student's achievement level changed from one year's test to the next. Because each student is compared with their own earlier result, much of the difference in who enrolls drops out, though not all of it.

{{chart:growth_frp}}

By this measure, low-income students at most charters in and near Minneapolis (29 of the 38 with enough students) made more progress in math than low-income students in MPS, who averaged 0.92 progress points, against 1.46 for low-income students statewide. Some caution is needed. Holding a higher achievement level counts as progress, so the measure still leans toward students who start higher, and achievement levels are coarse, so a student can improve a lot without changing levels. Still, progress points compare students with their own past results, which makes them a better guide to what schools add than average scores alone.

## Private money

Charter schools file the federal Form 990 like other nonprofits, and many have a separate foundation ("Friends of ...") or building company that files its own. We read the most recent one or two returns for each charter and its related organizations, for fiscal years ending in 2023, 2024 or 2025 [3].

{{chart:gifts}}

What we counted as private gifts: donations from individuals, foundations and companies, fundraising events and federated campaigns such as United Way (Form 990, Part VIII, lines 1a, 1c and 1f). We left out government grants, since most charters report state and federal aid there, and gifts passed from a school's own foundation to the school, so they are not counted twice. Where a charter's foundation filed a return, its gifts are added in.

Some things to keep in mind:

- **One year can be unusual.** Academias La Paz reported $2.56M of private gifts for the year ending June 2024 (about $5,000 per student), after about $10,000 the year before (about $19 per student; both bars are in the chart), while its government grants fell by a similar amount. That looks like a change in how revenue was classified rather than a large new donation, but we have not confirmed it.
- **Gifts are not the only outside support.** Networks can receive help in kind, such as staff training or recruiting paid for by a national organization, which may not show up as a gift on the school's return.
- **Who gives is not public.** The 990 lists donor names only on Schedule B, which is not released for public charities. Foundation grant lists (from the funder's side) would show who funds Minneapolis charters.

## After high school

Most charters in and near Minneapolis stop at 8th grade; 19 of them graduated high school seniors in 2023 or 2024. Minnesota's longitudinal data system (SLEDS) follows graduates into college in any state, the same source used for MPS on the [Tests and college](outcomes.html) page [11].

{{chart:college}}

The high schools differ a lot. Nova Classical Academy, Higher Ground Academy and Great River School sent three-quarters or more of their graduates to college, mostly four-year colleges where the type is reported. Hiawatha Academies, Hmong College Prep Academy, Lincoln International and Metro Schools were close to MPS (60%), with Hiawatha and Metro Schools sending more of their graduates to two-year colleges. Several charter high schools are built for students who left or struggled in other schools, such as High School for Recording Arts, Avalon School and Career Pathways, and their college rates are much lower. Minnesota Transitions' graduates come mostly from its online schools.

## Executive pay

Form 990 also lists the pay of each charter's officers and best-paid staff. For the 46 charters whose returns name a school leader paid for a full year (an executive director, school director, principal, head of school or CEO), the leader's total pay ran from about $61,000 at the smallest schools to about $258,000, with a median of about $139,000 [3]. Where the latest return shows a leader paid well under a full salary, usually after a change in leadership, we used the year before. Jennings Community School lists only an interim director ($32,620), likely a partial year; it is shown with a note. The highest were at the largest networks, among them Minnesota Transitions, Hiawatha Academies, Prodeo Academy, Higher Ground Academy and KIPP Minnesota. For comparison, the MPS superintendent's contract paid a base salary of $266,000 in 2024-25, plus a car allowance, for a district many times the size of any charter [9].

{{chart:pay_size}}

These charts are mostly for curiosity. Leader pay rises with school size, and the test scores mostly track which students a school serves, so a pattern between the two would not show that one causes the other. Eleven other charters' returns list only board members or department heads, often because the school leader is not a board officer and was paid under the $150,000 threshold at which the IRS requires key employees to be listed; they are left out.

## Who owns the buildings

Minnesota charters cannot buy buildings with state money, so most lease. The law allows three kinds of landlord: an affiliated building company, a nonprofit the school sets up after six years with healthy finances that owns the building and serves only that school; another nonprofit, church or school district; or a private owner [14]. The state pays "lease aid" toward the rent, up to 90% of the cost or $1,314 per pupil unit, whichever is less, if the rent is judged reasonable [15].

We looked up the owner of record for the land under each charter building in county property records [13].

{{chart:owners}}

- **Inside Minneapolis**, 53% of charter students attend buildings owned by their school's building company, 26% buildings owned by private companies or trusts, 10% churches or a mosque, and 9% other nonprofits. Metro Schools' two schools at 620 Olson Memorial Highway (1,122 students) account for much of the private share: an LLC with a Portland, Oregon address bought that property in March 2023 for $18.8M [13].
- **Just outside the city**, 53% attend buildings owned by the school's building company and another 22% buildings owned by the school's foundation or a related nonprofit, such as Friends of Nova Classical Academy or Friends of Prodeo.
- **Privately owned buildings pay property tax; nonprofit-owned school buildings mostly do not.** In the county records, 20 of the 72 parcels are taxable [13].

Building companies borrow, mostly with tax-exempt bonds, and charge the school rent that covers the payments. Twenty-two charters in and near Minneapolis reported building companies, with combined liabilities of about $427M at their latest year-end, about $368M of it tax-exempt bonds, against about $393M in assets [3]. The largest were Hmong College Prep Academy's Hmong Education Reform Company ($77M), Spero Academy ($46M), Hiawatha Academies ($43M), Prairie Seeds Academy ($32M), Higher Ground Academy ($28M) and Seven Hills Preparatory Academy ($26M). Debts are larger than book assets so far, so most rent has gone to interest and repayment; equity would build as the bonds are paid off. While MPS weighs closing buildings (see [Buildings](facilities.html)), several charters nearby have taken on long-term building debt to stay or grow.

A district building belongs to the public. A building company's building belongs to a private nonprofit, even when public lease aid paid for much of it. If one is sold, nonprofit law requires the proceeds to stay in the nonprofit sector, chosen by the building company's board, rather than return to the state [16].

## Open questions to check

- **How many of each charter's students live in Minneapolis?** The state publishes residents by district only, not by charter. MDE's records of charter enrollment by resident district, or charters' own annual reports, would show how many Minneapolis residents each charter enrolls, and how many attend charters outside the city.
- **How much of the difference is the school?** Admission lottery records for oversubscribed charters, combined with student-level test data from MDE, would allow a lottery comparison, the strongest test of what a school adds. MDE also computes scale-score growth for each student (shown on the Minnesota Report Card), a finer measure than the progress points used here.
- **Which charters are growing, and which are near capacity?** Seats per building are not in the state files. Charter authorizer reports and building company bond documents would show capacity and expansion plans.
- **What happens to a building when a charter closes or moves?** A 2024 bill (HF 3481) would have sent sale proceeds of buildings paid for with lease aid back to the state; we could not confirm whether it passed [16]. Bond documents for each building company would show what happens to the property on default or closure.
- **How much lease aid does each charter get, and how much rent does it pay?** MDE lease aid reports and the charters' audited financial statements would show both.
- **Who funds them?** Grant lists from the largest education funders (for example national and local foundations that support charter networks) would show where the private money comes from.
